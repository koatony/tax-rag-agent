How to Use this Example
You already know what a tax return is, you’ve filed one. These examples build on that starting point by
showing what happens when a professional accountant prepares a return for someone with more
complicated finances: a business owner, a rental property, investments, and retirement accounts. Many
of the forms and terms below are things you would encounter if your financial life became more
complex.
Whenever you see a term in bold that you might not recognize, look for the blue “What does this
mean?” box immediately below it. A full glossary is also included at the end.

What does this mean?
Schedule C is a tax form attached to your main Form 1040 that reports income and
expenses from a business you own. Think of it as a profit-and-loss summary written in the
IRS’s language. Marcus files a Schedule C because he owns Creature Comforts as a sole
proprietor — meaning he owns it personally rather than through a corporation.
Gross revenue is the total money the business brought in before subtracting any expenses.
Marcus’s gross revenue ($191,400) is not his profit — the store’s actual taxable income will
be much lower once expenses are deducted.
Schedule E (rental property) and Schedule D (investments) work the same way — they are
separate pages attached to the same Form 1040, each dedicated to a different type of
income.

What does this mean?
Form 1098 is a form your mortgage lender mails to you each January. It shows how much
interest you paid on your home loan during the year. Mortgage interest is often deductible, so
the IRS requires lenders to report it.
Capital loss carryover: If you sell an investment (like stock) for less than you paid, that’s a
capital loss. The tax code lets you deduct up to $3,000 of losses per year against your
regular income. If your losses exceed that limit, the remainder “carries over” automatically to
next year’s return. Marcus has $990 left over from a prior year — it now reduces this year’s
taxable income.

Depreciation: When a business or landlord buys a long-lasting asset — like a building or
major equipment — they generally cannot deduct the full cost in the year of purchase.
Instead, the IRS requires them to spread that cost over many years. For residential rental
property, the deduction is spread over 27.5 years. The Riveras’ rental condo qualifies, but
they never claimed this deduction in prior years — meaning they’ve been paying more tax

What does this mean?
IRC §162(f): “IRC” stands for Internal Revenue Code — the complete set of U.S. federal tax
laws written by Congress. The § symbol means “section.” So IRC §162(f) is Section 162(f) of
the tax code, which specifically prohibits deducting fines paid to government agencies as
business expenses. When a CPA cites an IRC section, they are pointing to the exact law that
justifies a decision.
Depreciable basis: The value you use as your starting point for calculating depreciation. For
a rental property, it’s generally the purchase price of the building minus the value of the land.
Land is excluded because it does not wear out over time the way a building does.

What does this mean?
Form 3115 (Change in Accounting Method): If a landlord forgot to claim depreciation for
several years, they have two options: (1) amend each prior-year return individually, or (2) file
Form 3115, which lets them catch up on all missed depreciation at once on the current-year
return. Most CPAs prefer Form 3115 because it is faster and less disruptive than reopening
multiple past returns.

What does this mean?
AGI (Adjusted Gross Income): Your total income for the year minus specific “above-theline” deductions. You’ve seen this on your own Form 1040 — it’s the number on Line 11. AGI
matters because many tax benefits (like IRA deductions and certain credits) are reduced or
eliminated once your AGI exceeds a threshold.
Phase-out: Some tax benefits don’t have a hard cut-off. Instead they gradually shrink as
your income rises. For example, the IRA deduction begins phasing out for married filers at a
certain AGI level and disappears completely above a higher threshold. The AI flags this
because the exact math depends on where the Riveras’ AGI lands after the return is
complete.
Self-employment tax: When you work as an employee, your employer pays half of your
Social Security and Medicare taxes. When you’re self-employed — as Marcus is with his
store — you pay both halves yourself, totaling about 15.3% of your net business income. To
partially offset this extra burden, the IRS lets you deduct half of the self-employment tax
above the line, meaning it reduces your AGI before you even get to itemized or standard
deductions.

What does this mean?
Traditional IRA vs. Roth IRA: Both are individual retirement accounts that grow taxadvantaged. The difference is timing. With a traditional IRA, you may deduct your
contribution now and pay taxes when you withdraw in retirement. With a Roth IRA, you
contribute after-tax dollars now and pay nothing when you withdraw. Which is better depends
on whether your tax rate is higher now or in retirement — a judgment call the CPA helps the
client think through.
Above-the-line deduction: A deduction you can take regardless of whether you itemize or
take the standard deduction. Because it reduces your AGI, it’s particularly valuable — a
lower AGI can unlock other benefits like larger IRA deductions and certain credits.

Instructor note
In this example, the AI handles the mechanical tasks which include classifying documents,
extracting figures, and populating the schedules so the tax preparer can begin at the
analysis stage rather than the data-entry stage. The reflection questions are designed to
discuss the conceptual distinction between what AI automates and what requires a trained
professional’s judgment. Students who have filed their own (simple) returns will likely
recognize the W-2 and Form 1040 immediately, which gives them a useful anchor for
understanding why a business owner’s return is more complex.
